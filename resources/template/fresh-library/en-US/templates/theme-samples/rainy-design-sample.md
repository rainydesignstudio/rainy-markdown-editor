Have you ever directed a team of agents?

I sometimes miss leading a small project team. It was less like being a general and more like keeping several different people moving together. The work was tiring because each person needed something different.

Weekly reports, daily meetings and Scrum notes filled the calendar. Under the familiar terms, we were still trying to decide what mattered and what had to be finished next.

Agents wait for instructions and can finish repetitive work quickly. The tradeoff is a quieter workplace and a greater need to make the instructions explicit.

`Find images, rename files, fill tables, measure dimensions, translate, check omissions`: familiar office tasks. An agent can help with these, but someone still needs to check the result and decide what happens next.

---
Useful, I thought.

Would a former manager see only the time saved and miss the work that still needs a person?

A documented workflow helps. A file called `colleague.skill` does not contain an entire colleague.

Enough daydreaming.

---
Back to `finding images, renaming files, filling tables, measuring, translating and checking omissions`. These are the agents I can direct now. A clear task might look like this:
```
Open `xxxx/.csv`. Its product rows contain thumbnails corresponding to images under `xxxx/`.

Complete these steps in order:

1. Match each product image to its row thumbnail and rename it using that row's SKU.
2. Leave uncertain images unchanged. Highlight the corresponding rows in yellow and move those images to `xxxx/not_renamed/`.
3. Measure each successfully matched image and fill a new Dimensions column.
4. Create category directories under `xxxx/` using the table categories, then sort the images into them.
5. Make a translated copy of the table for the target language. Highlight uncertain translations in yellow.
6. List missing information separately, then save the table.
```
Then make tea or coffee, or spend a little time elsewhere. I leaned against the counter while the leaves slowly opened. Years ago, I used to think the older programmers in the office typed too slowly.

Rinse the leaves, warm the cup, pour, and take a sip.

Attention is limited. I wanted to learn that slower pace, but I still carried the project around in my head even when it was not mine to own.

I walked back to the desk.

The screen held a conversation and a report. The agent had answered, carefully and without ceremony. The work was there; the feeling of a colleague was not.

**It does not understand me as a person. It can still follow a clear instruction and do useful work.**

That night I drafted requirements, reviewed two iterations and queued the next changes. Version 0.2 was a starting point, followed by 0.2.1, 0.2.2 and 0.3.0. Each step needed a concrete result to check.
In the morning, a boat passed the window by the river. I pulled the blanket over and finally slept.

Ten minutes earlier, I had wanted the whole thing ready immediately: a complete product, tested this week, ready for someone else to try.
